\title[Detecting text-reading hallucinations for blind users]{Detecting Text-Reading Hallucinations in Vision-Language Descriptions of Cyrillic Signage, Packaging and Documents for Blind Users}

\abstract{Blind people increasingly rely on vision-language models (VLMs) to read signs, product labels and documents aloud, but a model that cannot read a word tends to produce a plausible one instead. We measure how often this happens for Ukrainian (Cyrillic) text and whether it can be detected at run time. We built a benchmark of \NImages{} synthetic, photograph-like images of signage, packaging and documents. Every character is annotated, together with its visibility in the frame. The design crosses image quality, framing (cropping by the frame edge, finger or glare occlusion) and the type of the distinctive name (a real word, an invented word, or a word one letter away from a real one). Four Claude models described each image for a blind user and, separately, transcribed it. Only \RangeHall\% of the quoted words were hallucinated, yet \RangeImg\% of the descriptions contained at least one hallucinated word, including \RangeCleanFull\% of descriptions of clean, fully framed images. Invented and near-word names were hallucinated at \RangeNameHall\%, against \RangeWordHall\% for common words. Near-words were normalised to the real word, letters specific to Ukrainian were replaced by Russian-like spellings, and text hidden by the frame was completed. Only \RangeHedgeHall\% of hallucinated words were hedged. Combining the model's own re-reading, an independent Tesseract OCR pass and agreement between models detected hallucinated words with an AUROC of \RangeAucComb{}, catching \RangeRecTen\% of them at a 10\% false-flag rate. We derive design recommendations for assistive reading applications.}

\keywords{blind and visually impaired users, vision-language models, hallucination detection, scene text reading, Cyrillic script, assistive technology}
