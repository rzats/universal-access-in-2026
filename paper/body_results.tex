\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{How often models assert text that is not there (RQ1)}\label{sec:rq1}

Table~\ref{tab:rates} summarises all \NImages{} images (\NFableImages{} for Fable). Measured per quoted word, hallucination is rare: \HallPHaikuDesc\% of the words Haiku quotes in its descriptions are misread or fabricated, \HallPSonnetDesc\% for Sonnet, \HallPOpusDesc\% for Opus and \HallPFableDesc\% for Fable. Measured per response, which is what a blind user experiences, it is common. A description contains at least one hallucinated word in \ImgPHaikuDesc\% (Haiku), \ImgPSonnetDesc\% (Sonnet), \ImgPOpusDesc\% (Opus) and \ImgPFableDesc\% (Fable) of the images. In \ImgUnhHaikuDesc--\ImgUnhOpusDesc\% of the images, at least one hallucinated word is stated without any hedging in its sentence, so the listener has no cue to doubt it. Hallucination is not confined to degraded photographs: even for clean, fully framed images, \ImgCleanFullHaiku\% (Haiku), \ImgCleanFullSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \ImgCleanFullOpus\% (Opus) of the descriptions contain a hallucinated word. Errors in digits matter most for safety, because they affect dates, doses, prices, phone and account numbers. At least one wrong digit group appears in \ImgDigitHaiku\%, \ImgDigitSonnet\% and \ImgDigitOpus\% of the descriptions, respectively. Reading coverage is high: the models quote \CovHaikuDesc--\CovSonnetDesc\% of the visible ground-truth words. Under-reading is therefore not the main failure.

\paragraph{Image quality and framing.} Figure~\ref{fig:cells} breaks down the response-level rate by quality and framing. Severe degradation is the dominant driver: per quoted word, Haiku's hallucination rate rises from \QualCleanHaiku\% on clean images to \QualSevereHaiku\% on severely degraded ones, Sonnet's from \QualCleanSonnet\% to \QualSevereSonnet\% and Opus's from \QualCleanOpus\% to \QualSevereOpus\%. Moderate blur does not raise the rate (\QualBlurHaiku\%, \QualBlurSonnet\%, \QualBlurOpus\%), which suggests that current models read moderately blurred print reliably. Occlusion by a finger or glare raises the rate further (Haiku \FrameOccHaiku\%, Sonnet \FrameOccSonnet\%, Opus \FrameOccOpus\%, against \FrameFullHaiku\%, \FrameFullSonnet\% and \FrameFullOpus\% for full frames). A crop by the frame edge adds few misreadings but produces completions (Section~\ref{sec:completion}). Signage is the hardest genre (Haiku \GenreSignageHaiku\%, Sonnet \GenreSignageSonnet\%, Opus \GenreSignageOpus\% against \GenrePackagingHaiku\%, \GenrePackagingSonnet\% and \GenrePackagingOpus\% for packaging). Its names are set in the largest and most often decorative type, and a larger share of its text is names.

\paragraph{What kind of text is misread.} The category of the printed word matters far more than image quality (Figure~\ref{fig:categories}). Common words are hallucinated at \CatWordHaiku\% (Haiku), \CatWordSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \CatWordOpus\% (Opus), and real names at similar rates. Invented names, which no language prior can predict, are misread or replaced at \CatNonceHaiku\%, \CatNonceSonnet\% and \CatNonceOpus\%. Near-word names, one letter away from a real word, are misread at \CatLureHaiku\%, \CatLureSonnet\% and \CatLureOpus\%. Digits sit in between (\CatNumHaiku\%, \CatNumSonnet\%, \CatNumOpus\%). For the near-word names, the misreading is very often the real word: \LureLexHaiku\% (Haiku), \LureLexSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \LureLexOpus\% (Opus) of the claims matched to a near-word name are the lexicalised original. Examples are \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Метфермін»} read as \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Метформін»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Добробїт»} as \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Добробут»} and \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Воложка»} as \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Волошка»}. This is prior-driven normalisation: the model reports the word it expects rather than the word that is printed.

\paragraph{Ukrainian orthography.} A second systematic pattern is the loss of letters specific to Ukrainian. Among claims matched to invented names, \NonceUaHaiku\% (Haiku), \NonceUaSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \NonceUaOpus\% (Opus) differ from the printed form only in replacing \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ї, є} or \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ґ} by \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{і, е} or \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{г}, i.e., by spellings shared with Russian (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Пїжєназол»} read as \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Піженазол»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Ґражда»} as \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Гражда»}). Across all hallucinated words in descriptions, Ukrainian-letter substitutions account for \SubUaHaiku\% (Haiku), \SubUaSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \SubUaOpus\% (Opus). Digit errors account for \SubDigitHaiku\%, \SubDigitSonnet\% and \SubDigitOpus\%, lexicalisations for \SubLexHaiku\%, \SubLexSonnet\% and \SubLexOpus\%, and outright fabrications for \SubFabHaiku\%, \SubFabSonnet\% and \SubFabOpus\%. The remainder are other substitutions, such as grammatical ``corrections'' of the printed text, paraphrases inside quotation marks and wrong words in blurred small print.

\subsection{Completing text the camera did not capture}\label{sec:completion}

Crops and occlusions hide part of the text, and the description prompt often fills it in. Haiku produced \NComplHaiku{} completions of partly or wholly hidden words, Sonnet \NComplSonnet{} and Opus \NComplOpus{}. The constrained transcription prompt, which explicitly forbids completion and offers a placeholder, nearly eliminates them (\NComplTransHaiku, \NComplTransSonnet{} and \NComplTransOpus). Most completions in descriptions are correct guesses, because the hidden word is usually predictable (\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Шановні»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«температурі»}). They are also usually hedged: \ComplHedgedHaiku\%, \ComplHedgedSonnet\% and \ComplHedgedOpus\% of them appear in a sentence that flags uncertainty. The more capable models also frequently reason aloud about hidden content. For example, one model inferred a covered total from the visible line items of a receipt (``the three visible prices sum to 244.47, so the total is most likely 244.47''), which is helpful when right and dangerous when the hidden digit differs. In \IllegibleHaiku--\IllegibleSonnet\% of the descriptions the model said that some text was illegible, covered or cut off, so the models do report missing evidence. They still fill it in with plausible text in the same response.

\subsection{Hedging and the transcription prompt}\label{sec:hedging}

Hedging carries information, but not enough. Of the hallucinated words in descriptions, \HedgeHallHaiku\% (Haiku), \HedgeHallSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \HedgeHallOpus\% (Opus) are in a hedged sentence, compared with \HedgeCorrHaiku\%, \HedgeCorrSonnet\% and \HedgeCorrOpus\% of the correctly read words. The majority of hallucinations are therefore delivered with the same confidence as correct text, consistent with reports that verbal hedging is only loosely tied to model uncertainty~\cite{yona2024can}.

The transcription prompt suppresses completions but does not, in general, reduce misreadings. Per transcribed token, the hallucination rate is similar to or slightly higher than in descriptions (Table~\ref{tab:rates}), because transcriptions include more of the hard small print. Paired by image, the image-level outcome differs between the prompts for Haiku in \McDescHaiku{} versus \McTransHaiku{} images (description-only versus transcription-only hallucination; exact McNemar $p=\McPHaiku$), for Sonnet in \McDescSonnet{} versus \McTransSonnet{} ($p=\McPSonnet$) and for Opus in \McDescOpus{} versus \McTransOpus{} ($p=\McPOpus$). The ``(?)'' marks requested by the transcription prompt were used sparingly and are a weak detector on their own (Table~\ref{tab:detect}).

\subsection{Detecting hallucinated words (RQ2)}\label{sec:rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:detect} and Figure~\ref{fig:roc} report how well each run-time signal separates hallucinated from correct quoted words in descriptions. Agreement with the other models is the strongest single signal (AUROC \AucXmodelHaiku, \AucXmodelSonnet{} and \AucXmodelOpus{} for Haiku, Sonnet and Opus descriptions). The model's own re-reading under the transcription prompt (\AucRereadHaiku, \AucRereadSonnet, \AucRereadOpus) and the Tesseract cross-check (\AucOcrHaiku, \AucOcrSonnet, \AucOcrOpus) follow. Tesseract alone reads Ukrainian poorly on degraded images, but it does not share the VLM's language prior, so its disagreement is informative precisely where the VLM normalises. Lexicon membership (\AucLexHaiku, \AucLexSonnet, \AucLexOpus) and hedging cues (\AucHedgeHaiku, \AucHedgeSonnet, \AucHedgeOpus) are weak. The lexicon fails because hallucinations are often real words, the lexicalisation effect seen above.

The signals are complementary. The cross-validated combination reaches AUROC \AucCombCIHaiku{} for Haiku, \AucCombCISonnet{} for Sonnet and \AucCombCIOpus{} for Opus, with average precision of \ApHaiku, \ApSonnet{} and \ApOpus{} against base rates of \BaseHaiku\%, \BaseSonnet\% and \BaseOpus\%. At a false-flag rate of 10\% on correct words, it catches \RecTenHaiku\%, \RecTenSonnet\% and \RecTenOpus\% of hallucinated words. At 5\% it catches \RecFiveHaiku\%, \RecFiveSonnet\% and \RecFiveOpus\%. A training-free rule also works well. Flagging a quoted word when the model's own re-reading lacks it, or when neither OCR nor a majority of the other models confirms it, catches \RuleRecHaiku\%, \RuleRecSonnet\% and \RuleRecOpus\% of hallucinated words while flagging \RuleFfHaiku\%, \RuleFfSonnet\% and \RuleFfOpus\% of correct ones. Detection works equally well for digit tokens (combined AUROC \AucDigitsHaiku, \AucDigitsSonnet, \AucDigitsOpus), where errors matter most. Including completions as positives changes little (\AucUnsupHaiku, \AucUnsupSonnet, \AucUnsupOpus). At the image level, the maximum word score ranks images with at least one hallucination above clean ones with AUROC \ImgAucHaiku, \ImgAucSonnet{} and \ImgAucOpus.
