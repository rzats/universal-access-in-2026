\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the error profile means for blind users}

The headline token-level rates of a few per cent understate the accessibility problem. A blind user does not receive tokens but descriptions. Between \RangeImg\% of the synthetic descriptions and \RealRangeImg\% of the descriptions of real packaging and documents contained at least one word that is not printed or not visible. Most of these words were delivered without any cue of uncertainty. The errors also concentrate where they hurt. Names and rare words, which a language prior cannot predict, are hallucinated more often than common words in both studies, and they are what a user needs to tell one medicine, bill or product from another. Near-words are reported as the real word they resemble, on synthetic labels and on real official documents alike. A user told that a leaflet says ``\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{тяжким}'' has no way to learn that it says something else. The rare case in which an unusual printed string matters is the case the models are least likely to report faithfully.

Two further patterns are specific to the Ukrainian setting. First, letters that distinguish Ukrainian from Russian orthography are sometimes replaced by spellings shared with Russian. This happens on real medicine leaflets too, although less often than our stress test with synthetic invented names suggests. The change is easy to miss in a screen-reader rendering, but it alters the spelling of names that users may have to repeat, type or search for. Second, the models describe text that is cut off by the frame edge or covered by a finger by completing it. Completions are usually hedged and often right, but they are not readings. Because aiming errors are frequent in photographs taken by blind people~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz}, a description that fills in the unseen part removes the cue that would prompt the user to re-take the photograph.

Within the one model family we tested, capability is no remedy. The most expensive model, Fable~5.1, produced a hallucinated word in significantly more descriptions than the mid-range models in both studies. It tends to say more and to complete more of what is hidden, and all four models share the prior-driven normalisation of unfamiliar words. Whether the same holds for other vendors' models is open. Within this family, choosing a larger model does not replace a run-time check.

\subsection{Design recommendations}

The results suggest the following measures for assistive applications that read text with models of this kind:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Re-read before reading out.} A second, constrained transcription call by the same model is the cheapest single check. For Haiku it adds \RealCostHaikuTrans{}~USD and about \RealCostHaikuTransSec{}~s per real image (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Comparing the two readings word by word catches \RangeRereadRec\% of the hallucinated words in Study~1. Adding an on-device OCR pass, which shares no language prior with the model, and a second model where available raises this to \RangeRuleRec\% at a false-flag rate of \RangeRuleFf\%. On real images the gains are smaller (Table~\ref{tab:realdetect}), and a second model at least doubles cost and latency.
\item \emph{Mark, do not hide.} Flagged words should be voiced with an explicit marker (e.g., ``probably \ldots'') or spelled out letter by letter, rather than removed. This lets the user decide whether to re-take the photograph or seek sighted verification, in line with the verification strategies blind users already employ~\cite{alharbi2024misfitting,xie2025beyond}.
\item \emph{Separate reading from inference.} Prompts and interfaces should distinguish ``the label says'' from ``the hidden part is probably''. Our transcription prompt shows that completions can be almost eliminated by instruction. The description prompt should therefore ask the model to state explicitly where the frame cuts the text, which also tells the user how to move the camera.
\item \emph{Treat names and numbers as critical.} Digits and names should always pass the cross-check and, when flagged, be offered for character-level reading.
\end{enumerate}
These recommendations follow from measured detector performance, not from tests with users. Whether they improve decisions is a question for a user study (below).

\subsection{Limitations}

\emph{One vendor.} All evaluated models are Claude models of one vendor, queried through its command-line client with default settings. No statement in this paper is about vision-language models in general. Other model families, decoding settings and on-device models may behave differently. In particular, the cross-model detector may be optimistic here, because the peer models share a vendor and plausibly some of their errors. With independent vendors' models it may perform worse or better. A multi-vendor replication on the released stimuli is the most important next step. Fable~5.1 received only the description prompt, so it is not part of the detection analysis.

\emph{Synthetic versus real stimuli.} Study~1 is fully synthetic. Section~\ref{sec:setcompare} quantifies how it differs from real text: about six times fewer words per image, fewer rare words, more digits, and invented names that over-represent \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ї}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{є} and especially \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ґ} relative to natural Ukrainian. Its absolute rates, in particular the Ukrainian-letter error rate, are therefore not estimates for everyday text. Study~2 replaces the content with real packaging and documents, but its degradations (blur, cropping, finger and glare) are still applied synthetically. Its documents are renderings of PDF pages laid on a surface, without folds, curvature or real lighting. Its packaging photographs were taken by sighted contributors and are better framed than typical photographs taken by blind people~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. The near-word edit is typeset in a generic serif face, and an attentive reader may notice it.

\emph{Ground truth.} Each Study~2 genre has its own reference: Google Cloud Vision OCR checked against an independent contributor transcription for packaging, and the PDF text layer checked against Tesseract for documents. Both can miss text that is drawn rather than typeset (logos, stamps) or misread decorative type, and a model that correctly reads such text is scored as fabricating. Signage photographs were collected but excluded, because no reference independent of the evaluated model family was available. The automated audit indicates how large the resulting overstatement is.

\emph{Labelling and audit.} Claimed text is identified by quotation marks, which the prompt requested. Text a model reads out without quoting it is not scored, and quoted text that a model presents as an alternative reading is scored as a claim. The audit used two automated raters, not people. One rater belongs to the evaluated family, and the other, an open 12-billion-parameter model, could not resolve single-letter differences in Ukrainian and agreed poorly with the first. A human audit by Ukrainian readers remains necessary for a validated error rate.

\emph{Stability and scale.} Individual responses vary between runs (Section~\ref{sec:stability}), so per-image results are noisy. Aggregate rates are stable, but the run-to-run check covered only \StabN{} images. Study~2 has fewer than 20 images per model in each (quality, framing, genre) cell, so its condition-level comparisons are underpowered and mostly not significant after correction.

\emph{No user study.} We measure what is said, not how blind users perceive or act on it. Hallucination rates are proxies for the risk of acting on wrong information, not measures of harm. How flagged words should be voiced, and whether flags improve decisions without eroding trust in correct readings, requires a study with blind participants using screen readers in realistic reading tasks. Task-based usability instruments of the kind developed for adapted reading materials could support such a study~\cite{zatserkovnyi2023usability}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Four Claude models read Ukrainian signs, labels and documents well enough that per-word error rates are low. They nevertheless assert text that is not printed in a large share of their descriptions: in \RangeImg\% of descriptions of synthetic stimuli and \RealRangeImg\% of descriptions of real packaging and documents. The main mechanisms are normalising unusual words to familiar ones, which we reproduced on real official documents with a one-letter edit, altering digits, completing text the camera did not capture and, less often on real text, replacing Ukrainian-specific letters. Most of these assertions carry no hedge, and the most capable model of the family is not the most faithful reader. The errors are detectable at run time. A second constrained reading, an independent OCR pass and agreement between models together separate hallucinated from correct words with an AUROC of \RangeAucComb{} on synthetic and \RealRangeAucComb{} on real images. Assistive applications can therefore tell blind users \emph{which} words to doubt. Whether these findings hold for other vendors' models, and whether such flags help users, remains to be tested. The stimulus generator, source lists, ground truth and analysis code are released to support both.
