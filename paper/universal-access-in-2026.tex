\title[Detecting text-reading hallucinations for blind users]{Detecting Text-Reading Hallucinations in Vision-Language Descriptions of Cyrillic Signage, Packaging and Documents for Blind Users}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Rostyslav} \sur{Zatserkovnyi}}\email{zatserkovnyi.rostyslav@gmail.com}

\author[2]{\fnm{Roksoliana} \sur{Zatserkovna}}\email{roksoliana.s.zatserkovna@lpnu.ua}

\affil*[1]{\orgname{Lviv University of Trade and Economics}, \orgaddress{\street{10 Tuhan-Baranovskyi Str.}, \city{Lviv}, \postcode{79008}, \country{Ukraine}}}

\affil[2]{\orgname{Lviv Polytechnic National University}, \orgaddress{\street{12 Stepan Bandera Str.}, \city{Lviv}, \postcode{79013}, \country{Ukraine}}}

\abstract{Blind people increasingly rely on vision-language models to read signs, product labels and documents aloud, but a model that cannot read a word tends to produce a plausible one instead. We measure how often this happens for Ukrainian (Cyrillic) text with four Claude models of one vendor, and whether it can be detected at run time. Study~1 uses \NImages{} synthetic images in a factorial design that crosses image quality, framing (cropping, finger or glare occlusion) and the type of the distinctive name (real word, invented word, near-word). Study~2 uses \RealNImages{} real images, namely Open Food Facts packaging photographs and official Ukrainian documents, with ground truth independent of the evaluated models. Only \RangeHall\% (Study~1) and \RealRangeHall\% (Study~2) of quoted words were hallucinated. An automated audit confirmed most hallucination labels in Study~1 but only about half in Study~2, where the reference text misses some printed content. The audit-adjusted rates are therefore \SynAdjRange\% and \RealAdjRange\%. Yet \RangeImg\% and \RealRangeImg\% of descriptions contained at least one word labelled as hallucinated, mostly without hedging. In a paired probe on real documents, a printed near-word was reported as the real word it resembles in \RangePrNorm\% of pages. Within the family, the most expensive model was not the most faithful reader. Combining the model's own re-reading, an independent OCR pass and agreement between models detected hallucinated words with an AUROC of \RangeAucComb{} (Study~1) and \RealRangeAucComb{} (Study~2). We report paired tests, run-to-run stability, cost and latency, and an audit of the labeller by two automated raters, and we derive design recommendations for assistive reading applications.}

\keywords{blind and visually impaired users, vision-language models, hallucination detection, scene text reading, Cyrillic script, assistive technology}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Reading text is one of the most frequent reasons blind people photograph their surroundings. The first crowd-powered visual question answering service, VizWiz, already found that a large share of questions asked what a label, sign or screen said~\cite{bigham2010vizwiz}, and later analyses of tens of thousands of such questions confirmed that reading and product identification dominate everyday visual needs~\cite{brady2013visual,gurari2018vizwiz}. Dedicated text readers such as Seeing AI and OrCam serve this need~\cite{granquist2021evaluation,khan2020insight}, and image-processing methods can adapt other visual content, such as illustrations, for low-vision readers~\cite{zatserkovnyi2024adapting}. Since 2023, general-purpose vision-language models (VLMs) have increasingly taken over the task. Scene-description apps built on these models let a blind user point a phone at a medicine box or a utility bill and receive a fluent spoken description~\cite{gonzalezpenuela2024investigating,xie2025beyond,adnin2024king}.

Fluency is the problem. A conventional OCR engine that cannot read a word usually produces visible garbage, and a sighted user would notice it. A VLM that cannot read a word tends to produce a \emph{plausible} word instead: a common brand name in place of an unusual one, a complete street name where the sign is cut off by the edge of the frame, or a date that is one digit off. For a blind user the description is often the only access to the text. Blind users are known to place considerable trust in machine-generated descriptions even when they are wrong~\cite{macleod2017understanding}, and they have few independent ways to check them~\cite{alharbi2024misfitting,hong2024understanding}. A hallucinated expiry date, dosage or amount due is therefore not a cosmetic error but an accessibility failure with practical consequences.

This risk is plausibly greater for Cyrillic text, and for Ukrainian in particular. Text-centric benchmarks report clearly lower VLM accuracy for non-Latin scripts~\cite{liu2024ocrbench,tang2025mtvqa}. Ukrainian adds letters that do not exist in Russian (\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{і, ї, є, ґ}) and an apostrophe inside words, so a model with a strong Russian or generic Cyrillic prior can silently ``correct'' them. Ukrainian everyday text is also dense with proper names, such as brands, surnames, street names and invented shop names, which a language prior cannot predict.

This paper asks two questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] When a VLM describes a photograph of Cyrillic signage, packaging or a document for a blind user, how often does it assert text that is not printed there, and what drives these text-reading hallucinations?
\item[RQ2] Can such hallucinations be detected automatically, without ground truth, using signals that an assistive application could compute at run time?
\end{description}

Answering RQ1 requires knowing exactly what is printed and what is actually visible in the photograph. We address it in two studies. Study~1 is a controlled factorial experiment on \NImages{} synthetic, photograph-like images of Ukrainian signs, labels and documents. Every character and its visibility are known, and the design crosses image quality, framing (cropping by the frame edge, finger or glare occlusion) and the type of the distinctive name: a real word, an invented word, or a \emph{near-word} one letter away from a real word. Study~2 repeats the measurements on \RealNImages{} images of real Ukrainian text. These are \NPack{} packaging photographs from Open Food Facts and \NDoc{} official and public documents, such as Pension Fund and city-council forms, Ministry of Health medical forms, utility bills, bank forms, medicine leaflets and notices. Each has a ground truth that is independent of the evaluated models, and each is degraded and framed in the same way as in Study~1. A paired near-word probe edits one word on real document pages.

We evaluated four Claude models of one vendor (Haiku~5.5, Sonnet~5.5, Opus~5.5 and Fable~5.1) with a blind-user description prompt and a constrained transcription prompt. Every word the models quote as printed text was labelled automatically against the ground truth. The labeller was checked by two independent automated raters. The study is a comparison within one model family; it makes no claim about vision-language models in general.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a synthetic factorial benchmark and a real-image benchmark of Ukrainian packaging and documents, both with character-level visibility ground truth, released with code, source lists and response-free results;
\item an error taxonomy grounded in visibility, which separates misreadings and fabrications of visible text from \emph{completions} of text the camera did not capture, with prevalence per model and condition;
\item evidence, on synthetic and on real documents, that the models normalise unfamiliar printed words to familiar ones, and measurements of how image quality, framing and word category drive errors, with paired tests across models;
\item an evaluation of run-time detectors with operating points, costs and latencies, and an assessment of run-to-run stability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{AI visual assistance for blind and low-vision people}

Remote sighted assistance and crowd-sourced answers~\cite{bigham2010vizwiz} were followed by datasets of real photographs taken by blind people, which made their characteristics measurable: VizWiz images are frequently blurred, poorly framed or partly occluded, and many of them contain text~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz,gurari2020captioning}. What blind and low-vision people want from image descriptions depends on the context, but transcribing visible text is consistently valued~\cite{stangl2020person}. Earlier computer-vision aids for visually impaired people focused on detection and recognition pipelines~\cite{zatserkovnyi2021cv,khan2020insight}. Text readers built on such pipelines are now an established tool, although their accuracy degrades on real-world photographs~\cite{granquist2021evaluation}.

Large multimodal models changed the interaction from reading out recognised text to conversational description. Diary and interview studies of apps such as Be My AI report broad adoption and new use cases, alongside errors that users find hard to detect and strategies for verifying output, such as re-taking photographs or asking again~\cite{gonzalezpenuela2024investigating,xie2025beyond,adnin2024king}. Studies of how blind people handle recognition errors show that verification is laborious and often impossible without sighted help~\cite{alharbi2024misfitting,hong2024understanding}. Blind users also tend to trust captions that are inconsistent with the context~\cite{macleod2017understanding}. These findings motivate detection that works \emph{inside} the assistive pipeline rather than relying on the user.

\subsection{Hallucination in vision-language models}

Object hallucination in image captioning was quantified early with the CHAIR metric, which attributed it partly to language priors~\cite{rohrbach2018object}. For instruction-tuned VLMs it has been probed with polling questions in POPE~\cite{li2023evaluating} and with adversarial visual questions in HallusionBench~\cite{guan2024hallusionbench}. Recent surveys organise the causes into data, model and decoding factors, and highlight the dominance of the language prior when visual evidence is weak~\cite{bai2024hallucination,liu2024survey}. We use the same intuition but measure it on text, where the visual evidence can be degraded in a controlled way. Our earlier work on inclusive image captioning with large language models~\cite{zatserkovnyi2025llm} examined description quality for visually impaired users. The present study isolates the text-reading component of such descriptions.

\subsection{Reading text with vision-language models}

Scene-text benchmarks such as TextVQA~\cite{singh2019towards}, ST-VQA~\cite{biten2019scene} and TextCaps~\cite{sidorov2020textcaps} established that answering and captioning often require reading. OCRBench showed that large multimodal models rely on semantic plausibility: they read meaningful words far better than non-semantic character strings~\cite{liu2024ocrbench}. MTVQA found a substantial accuracy gap for non-Latin scripts~\cite{tang2025mtvqa}. On food packaging, a controlled comparison under realistic print degradations found that vision-language models extract structured label information better than classical OCR, but that a small model exhibits a hallucination mode with high completeness and low accuracy~\cite{zatserkovna2026packaging}. Text in an image also strongly steers model behaviour, as typographic attacks show~\cite{cheng2024unveiling}. Closest to our work, recent studies document OCR hallucination by multimodal models on degraded documents~\cite{he2025seeing} and fluent fabrication by VLM-based OCR on historical documents even where the character error rate is low~\cite{gardella2026low}. None of these studies controls what is visible in the image or targets the assistive setting, Cyrillic script or Ukrainian orthography. We use synthetic rendering, a standard way to obtain exact text ground truth~\cite{jaderberg2016reading,gupta2016synthetic}, to provide this control.

\subsection{Detecting hallucinations without ground truth}

Black-box detectors exploit the observation that hallucinated content is unstable across samples: SelfCheckGPT compares a response with stochastic re-samples~\cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt}, and semantic entropy measures disagreement among sampled meanings~\cite{kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024detecting}. Models can also be asked to state their confidence~\cite{lin2022teaching,kadavath2022language}. However, verbalised confidence tends to be overconfident~\cite{xiong2023can}, and hedging language does not faithfully reflect a model's intrinsic uncertainty~\cite{yona2024can}. For text reading, an independent OCR engine such as Tesseract~\cite{smith2007overview} offers a cross-check that does not share the VLM's language prior. Our study compares these families of signals on the same quoted words.


\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

The study has two parts. Study~1 uses a fully controlled synthetic factorial design. Study~2 measures the same quantities on real Ukrainian packaging photographs and documents, and adds a near-word probe on real document pages. The models, prompts, labelling and detectors are shared.

\subsection{Study 1: synthetic factorial stimuli}\label{sec:synth}

\paragraph{Content.} A generator written for this study produces Ukrainian text in three genres that blind users commonly need to read. \emph{Signage} covers shop signs with opening hours, street-name plates, notices and public-transport stops. \emph{Packaging} covers food labels with net weight, ingredients, best-before date and manufacturer, medicine boxes with dose, count, expiry date and batch number, and household chemicals with hazard warnings. \emph{Documents} cover utility bills, shop receipts, medical referrals and building announcements. Content is assembled from curated Ukrainian vocabulary (e.g., 18 shop types, 30 real street names, 18 cities, real first names and patronymics) and random numerals (dates, times, prices, phone numbers, account and batch numbers). Each ground-truth token carries the category of the slot that produced it: \emph{common word}, \emph{real name}, \emph{invented name}, \emph{near-word name} or \emph{digits}.

Every stimulus has one distinctive \emph{name slot}: a brand, shop, company, street or patient surname, or a drug name. Its content is set by a balanced factor with three levels. In the \emph{word} level it is a real word or name typical of Ukrainian brands (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Ластівка»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Барвінок»}) or a real surname or drug name. In the \emph{invented} level it is a pronounceable string of Ukrainian syllables that is absent from a 290-thousand-word Ukrainian frequency list derived from OpenSubtitles\footnote{\url{https://github.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords} (2018 release, CC BY-SA 4.0).} (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Фецедвус»}). In the \emph{near-word} level it is a real word with exactly one letter replaced by another letter of the same class, again checked to be absent from the word list (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Воложка»} from \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Волошка»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Метфермін»} from \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Метформін»}). The near-word level is the sharpest probe of prior-driven normalisation: a model that reads what it expects rather than what is printed will ``correct'' it. Synthetic rendering gives complete control over these factors and an exact, independent ground truth. Its realism is limited, however, and Study~2 therefore repeats the measurements on real images (the two sets are compared in Section~\ref{sec:setcompare}).

\paragraph{Rendering.} Lines are typeset on a panel styled for the genre: coloured sign plates with optional frames, pale product labels, or white and off-white paper, the latter with a monospaced font for receipts. Nineteen Cyrillic-capable font families are used, including serif, sans-serif, condensed, monospaced and decorative or handwritten faces; decorative faces appear only in titles. The panel is placed in a textured background scene through a random homography with small perspective distortion and rotation, so that it resembles a hand-held photograph. Because every character's box is known in panel coordinates, its position in the final image is known exactly.

\paragraph{Framing.} The second factor controls what reaches the camera. \emph{Full frame} keeps the whole panel with a margin. \emph{Crop} moves one frame edge (left, right or bottom) into the panel by 14--35\%, which cuts lines and words as happens when a blind user aims a phone imprecisely~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. \emph{Occlusion} places either a skin-coloured finger entering from the bottom edge or a specular glare highlight over a random character of a random line. For each character we test a $3\times3$ grid of points inside its box. A character is \emph{visible} if at least 8 of the 9 points are inside the frame and not occluded, \emph{hidden} if at most 2 are, and \emph{partial} otherwise. For glare, a point counts as occluded where the highlight's opacity exceeds 0.85. A token's state follows from its characters: visible, hidden, or partial if its characters are mixed. Crop and occlusion stimuli were re-drawn until at least one token was not fully visible, and full-frame stimuli until none was hidden.

\paragraph{Quality.} The third factor degrades the image after it is scaled to 1024~pixels on the long side. \emph{Clean} adds mild sensor noise and JPEG compression at quality 90. \emph{Blur} applies a Gaussian blur with $\sigma\in[1.6,2.4]$~px, reduces contrast to 80--95\% and compresses at quality 75. \emph{Severe} applies $\sigma\in[2.8,3.8]$~px, reduces contrast to 50--70\% and brightness to 65--90\%, adds stronger noise and compresses at quality 45. Small print becomes illegible under severe degradation while titles remain readable, which reproduces the graded legibility of real photographs taken by blind users.

\paragraph{Design.} The full factorial design crosses genre (3) $\times$ quality (3) $\times$ framing (3) $\times$ name-slot level (3), giving 81 cells with \NImgPerCell{} images per cell, \NImages{} images in total. The images contain \NGTTokens{} ground-truth tokens, of which \NGTNotVisible{} are not fully visible. Figure~\ref{fig:examples_synth} shows examples. The generator is seeded per stimulus, so the image set is reproducible byte for byte from the released code.

\subsection{Study 2: real-image benchmark}\label{sec:stimuli}

All Study~2 stimuli are photographs or renderings of real Ukrainian objects and documents that a Ukrainian reader would recognise. Only the photographic conditions are manipulated synthetically (framing and image quality, below), and a single word is edited in the near-word probe (Section~\ref{sec:probe}). The benchmark has two genres, each with its own source of ground truth that is independent of the evaluated models. Signage could not be included in Study~2. We collected 70 freely licensed Wikimedia Commons photographs of Ukrainian signs, but the only available transcription of them was produced with a Claude model, a member of the evaluated family, so it is not independent of the systems under test. Signage is therefore covered by Study~1 only.

\paragraph{Packaging.} \NPack{} raw photographs of Ukrainian food packaging (ingredient panels and fronts) were taken from Open Food Facts, a collaborative database whose contributors photograph products in shops and at home. Next to each raw photograph, the database stores a Google Cloud Vision OCR result with a bounding box for every character, and this OCR is the ground truth. To make sure the OCR is reliable, an ingredient photograph is kept only if at least 60\% of the words in the contributor's independent transcription of the ingredients occur in the OCR. A front photograph is kept only if at least half of the words of the contributor's product name occur in it. The contributor's transcription, product name and brand are added as further accepted reference text.

\paragraph{Documents.} \NDocsCollected{} real documents were collected as PDF files with a text layer from official and public Ukrainian sources. They include application forms of the Pension Fund, the Ministry of Justice, the State Land Cadastre and city councils' administrative-service centres (ЦНАП); Ministry of Health medical forms (e.g., 086/о, 027/о, 106/о); patient leaflets of medicines; electricity and gas bills and tariff notices from regional suppliers and the energy regulator; bank application forms and consumer-credit passports; university and school documents; announcements of councils and the National Police; and pages of library bulletins with book annotations, the closest available text to the back cover of a book. For each document, a block of consecutive lines with up to 150 words was cut from the first page (from the richest of pages 2--4 for library bulletins, whose first page is a cover). It was rendered at 150~dpi, and the text layer, with word boxes split into character boxes, is the ground truth. The rendered page is then ``photographed'' as a sheet lying on a textured surface, with a random homography (small perspective distortion and rotation) and uneven lighting. As a quality gate, Tesseract must find at least 60\% of the text-layer words on the clean rendering. This rejected \NDocsRejected{} documents, e.g., bilingual forms and PDFs whose text layer did not match the visible glyphs. \NDoc{} documents remain.

\paragraph{Framing.} Each stimulus is assigned one of three framing conditions. \emph{Full frame} keeps the whole object. \emph{Crop} moves one frame edge (left, right or bottom) into the text area by 14--35\%, which cuts lines and words as happens when a blind user aims a phone imprecisely~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. \emph{Occlusion} places either a skin-coloured finger entering from the bottom edge or a specular glare highlight over a randomly chosen character. Because the position of every character is known, its visibility in the final frame can be computed. We test a $3\times3$ grid of points inside each character box. A character is \emph{visible} if at least 8 of the 9 points are inside the frame and not occluded, \emph{hidden} if at most 2 are, and \emph{partial} otherwise. For glare, a point counts as occluded where the highlight's opacity exceeds 0.85. A token is visible, hidden, or partial if its characters are mixed. 

\paragraph{Quality.} All stimuli are then scaled to 1024~pixels on the long side and assigned one of three quality levels. \emph{Clean} adds only mild sensor noise. \emph{Blur} applies a Gaussian blur with $\sigma\in[1.6,2.4]$~px and reduces contrast to 80--95\%. \emph{Severe} applies $\sigma\in[2.8,3.8]$~px, reduces contrast to 50--70\% and brightness to 65--90\%, and adds stronger noise. Images are stored as JPEG at the highest quality that keeps the file below 240~kB, because the model client silently drops larger image attachments. Within each genre, the nine (quality, framing) cells are balanced and assigned in a seeded random order.

\paragraph{Ground-truth categories.} Every ground-truth token is categorised as \emph{digits}, a \emph{common word} (present in a 290-thousand-word Ukrainian frequency list derived from OpenSubtitles\footnote{\url{https://github.com/hermitdave/FrequencyWords} (2018 release, CC BY-SA 4.0).}), or a \emph{name or rare word} (absent from it: brands, surnames, place names, technical terms). The benchmark contains \RealNImages{} images with \RealNGTTokens{} ground-truth tokens, of which \RealNGTNotVisible{} are not fully visible. Figure~\ref{fig:examples} shows examples.

\subsection{Near-word probe}\label{sec:probe}

Prior-driven normalisation, where a model reports the word it expects rather than the word that is printed, cannot be measured on unedited text, because the printed and expected words coincide. We therefore edited the real document pages minimally. On each page, one common word of 6--12 letters that occurs once on the page was chosen at random. It was overpainted with a \emph{near-word}: the same word with one interior letter replaced by another letter of the same class (vowel or consonant), checked to be absent from the word list (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«документ»}~$\to$~\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«докуцент»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«сплатити»}~$\to$~\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«сзлатити»}). The near-word was typeset in a serif face at the original word's height and width, in the sampled ink colour. Each edited page was paired with its unedited original, and both were photographed with identical framing (full) and identical random degradation (clean or blur). Pages where the target word would be smaller than 11~px in the final image were skipped, leaving \NProbePairs{} pairs. In the description of each image we check whether the target is quoted in its printed form, whether the real word is quoted instead (normalisation), or whether some other form is quoted.

\subsection{Models and prompts}\label{sec:models}

We queried four production models of a single vendor, Anthropic, spanning its capability and price range: Claude Haiku~5.5, Sonnet~5.5, Opus~5.5 and Fable~5.1. All four were run on all \NImages{} Study~1 images, all \RealNImages{} Study~2 images and all probe pairs. The design is therefore a comparison \emph{within one model family}, and none of our findings should be read as a statement about vision-language models in general. For cost reasons Fable~5.1, the most expensive model, received only the description prompt, and only descriptions were collected in the probe. To measure run-to-run stability, the description prompt was run twice more, on one Study~1 image per design cell (\StabN{} images), for every model. All calls used the vendor's command-line client in non-interactive mode with default sampling settings, no tools and a short system prompt in Ukrainian (all prompts are given verbatim in Appendix~\ref{secA1}): \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Ти — сервіс опису зображень для незрячих і слабозорих користувачів. Відповідай українською мовою.»} (``You are an image-description service for blind and low-vision users. Answer in Ukrainian.''). Images were passed under opaque hashed file names so that the file name could not reveal the condition.

Two user prompts were used, each in a separate call without shared context. The \emph{description} prompt imitates a blind user's request: \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Я незряча людина. Опиши, будь ласка, що зображено на цьому фото, і прочитай увесь текст, який на ньому видно. Увесь прочитаний текст наводь дослівно в лапках «…».»} (``I am a blind person. Please describe what is shown in this photo and read all the text that is visible on it. Give all the text you read verbatim in «…» quotation marks.''). The request for guillemets makes the claimed text machine-extractable without an additional model. The \emph{transcription} prompt asks for a verbatim line-by-line transcription, forbids corrections and completions, asks for a placeholder \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{[нерозбірливо]} (``illegible'') where text is cut off, covered or unreadable, and asks for a ``(?)'' after any uncertain word. It serves both as a mitigation condition and as a run-time re-reading signal for detection.

\subsection{Extracting and labelling claimed text}\label{sec:labelling}

From each description we extract every guillemet-quoted span; an opening guillemet runs to the next closing one on the same line, so nested quotation marks cannot absorb descriptive prose. Straight and curly double quotes are also accepted. Bracketed or parenthesised remarks inside a quotation (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«Осо[закрито пальцем] рахунок»}) are treated as annotations and removed, as are quotations that consist only of meta-words such as ``covered'' or of a single letter. Duplicate spans within a response are counted once. From each transcription, all lines are used after removing placeholders. Text is normalised by lower-casing, unifying apostrophe variants and mapping Latin look-alike letters to Cyrillic. The mapping is applied to the reference, the model output and the OCR alike, so mixed-script PDF text layers compare correctly. It is then split into letter and digit tokens. Digits separated by dots, colons or commas are merged, so dates, times and prices compare as units. Phone-like digit runs are also merged regardless of spacing, and leading zeros are dropped.

Each claimed token $o$ receives one label from the ground truth of its image, including the accepted reference text:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{correct}: $o$ equals a visible token or a readable partial token, or $o$ is an explicit fragment (at least two characters) of a visible token or a contiguous run of the visible characters of a partial token;
\item \textbf{completion}: $o$ equals the full form of a token at least one of whose characters is hidden, i.e., the text is true but the camera did not capture it;
\item \textbf{misreading}: none of the above, but $o$ is within a normalised edit similarity of 0.5 of some ground-truth token, i.e., printed text was rendered wrongly;
\item \textbf{fabrication}: no ground-truth token is that similar, i.e., the text was invented.
\end{itemize}
A \emph{hallucinated} token is a misreading or a fabrication; \emph{unsupported} tokens additionally include completions. Misreadings are sub-typed as \emph{digit} errors, \emph{Ukrainian-letter} substitutions (the claimed and printed words differ only in \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{і/и, ї/і, є/е, ґ/г} or contain letters that exist only in Russian), \emph{lexicalisations} (the claimed form is a real word or a known name but the printed one is not), and other substitutions. We also record whether the sentence containing a quotation carries a hedging cue (e.g., \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«ймовірно»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«схоже»}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{«нечітко»}, ``probably'', ``looks like'', ``blurry''). Matching against the image's token set rather than a forced alignment is deliberately conservative: a wrong number that happens to occur elsewhere on the same page counts as correct. Conversely, text that is printed but absent from the reference (e.g., a word the reference OCR missed) is counted as a fabrication, so the audit below is essential.

\paragraph{Automated audit with two raters.} The labeller was audited by two independent automated raters, not by people. A stratified random sample of description tokens was drawn: \SynAudN{} from Study~1 (25 per model and label) and \RealAudN{} from Study~2 (15 per model and label, where available). Each rater received the stimulus image, the reference line the token was matched to, the quoted span, the token and the assigned label with its definition. It was asked (i) whether the label is right and (ii) whether the quoted word reproduces text that is printed and fully visible in the image. The second question is the one that matters for the main metric. A misreading or fabrication is confirmed if the word is judged \emph{not} printed-and-visible, a correct token if it is judged printed-and-visible, and a completion if it is judged not visible. The raters were Claude Sonnet~5.5 (through the same client as the evaluated runs) and the open model Gemma~3 12B (\texttt{gemma3:12b}, served locally by Ollama, temperature~0). Disagreements were resolved by a fixed conservative rule: an item counts as confirmed only if every rater that rated it confirms it. We report per-rater and resolved precision with Wilson intervals and Cohen's kappa~\cite{cohen1960coefficient} between the raters. This is agreement between automated raters, not human validation. One rater belongs to the evaluated model family, which may bias it towards the evaluated models' readings. The rater prompt is in Appendix~\ref{secA1}.

\subsection{Detectors}\label{sec:detectors}

For each token quoted in a description, five signals are computed without access to the ground truth:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{re-reading}: the token is absent from the same model's transcription of the same image; the variant \emph{re-reading uncertainty} fires when the transcription marks the token with ``(?)'';
\item \emph{cross-model}: the fraction of the other two main models whose description or transcription of the image does not contain the token;
\item \emph{OCR}: one minus the best normalised edit similarity between the token and any token Tesseract~5.5 (\texttt{tessdata\_best} Ukrainian model, page-segmentation modes 3 and 11 pooled) reads in the image~\cite{smith2007overview};
\item \emph{lexicon}: the token is not in the Ukrainian frequency list;
\item \emph{hedging}: the quoting sentence carries a hedging cue;
\item \emph{sample disagreement} (repeat subset only): the fraction of the model's two other descriptions of the same image that do not contain the token, a SelfCheckGPT-style signal~\cite{manakul2023selfcheckgpt}.
\end{enumerate}
Each signal is evaluated alone by the area under the ROC curve (AUROC)~\cite{hanley1982meaning}, and all signals are combined, together with the token's length and digit status, in a class-balanced logistic regression. Its scores are obtained by five-fold cross-validation with folds grouped by image, so that no image contributes to both training and testing. We report AUROC, average precision and, for deployment, the share of hallucinated tokens caught at fixed false-flag rates of 5, 10 and 20\% on correct tokens. We also evaluate a simple rule that needs no training: flag a token if the model's re-reading lacks it, or if neither OCR nor a majority of the other models confirms it.

\subsection{Statistics}\label{sec:stats}

Proportions are reported with Wilson 95\% score intervals~\cite{wilson1927probable}. Because tokens are clustered within images, we additionally report percentile intervals from a cluster bootstrap over images~\cite{efron1979bootstrap} (1000 resamples for rates and 300 for AUROC). Every comparison stated in the text is tested. Comparisons on shared images (model versus model, description versus transcription) use the exact McNemar test on the image-level outcome ``at least one hallucinated word'', together with a paired cluster-bootstrap 95\% interval (2000 resamples of images) for the difference in token-level hallucination rate. Comparisons between disjoint image groups (quality, framing, genre levels) use Fisher's exact test on the image-level outcome and an unpaired cluster-bootstrap interval for the token-rate difference. $p$-values are Holm-corrected within each family: the six model pairs per study, and the condition contrasts per study. All analysis code and the response-free aggregate results are released (see Declarations). Raw model responses are withheld from the repository but can be regenerated with the released scripts.

Ukrainian-letter substitutions account for \SubUaHaiku\% (Haiku), \SubUaSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \SubUaOpus\% (Opus), digit errors for \SubDigitHaiku\%, \SubDigitSonnet\% and \SubDigitOpus\%, lexicalisations for \SubLexHaiku\%, \SubLexSonnet\% and \SubLexOpus\%, and fabrications for \SubFabHaiku\%, \SubFabSonnet\% and \SubFabOpus\%. The remainder are other substitutions, such as grammatical ``corrections'', paraphrases inside quotation marks and wrong words in blurred small print.

\paragraph{Completions, hedging and the transcription prompt.}\label{sec:completion} Crops and occlusions hide part of the text, and the description prompt often fills it in. Haiku produced \NComplHaiku{} completions of partly or wholly hidden words, Sonnet \NComplSonnet{} and Opus \NComplOpus{}. The constrained transcription prompt, which explicitly forbids completion and offers a placeholder, nearly eliminates them (\NComplTransHaiku, \NComplTransSonnet{} and \NComplTransOpus). Completions in descriptions are usually hedged (\ComplHedgedHaiku\%, \ComplHedgedSonnet\% and \ComplHedgedOpus\%), whereas misreadings and fabrications mostly are not: \HedgeHallHaiku\% (Haiku), \HedgeHallSonnet\% (Sonnet) and \HedgeHallOpus\% (Opus) of the hallucinated words are in a hedged sentence, compared with \HedgeCorrHaiku\%, \HedgeCorrSonnet\% and \HedgeCorrOpus\% of the correctly read words. This is consistent with reports that verbal hedging is only loosely tied to model uncertainty~\cite{yona2024can}. Whether the transcription prompt reduces misreadings depends on the model. Paired by image, it lowers the image-level rate for Opus (\SynCOpusDescTransImg{} of images, Holm $p=\SynCOpusDescTransP$; token-rate difference \SynCOpusDescTransTok{} percentage points), but not for Haiku ($p=\SynCHaikuDescTransP$) or Sonnet ($p=\SynCSonnetDescTransP$).

\subsection{Study 1: detecting hallucinated words (RQ2)}\label{sec:rq2}

Table~\ref{tab:detect} and Figure~\ref{fig:roc} report how well each run-time signal separates hallucinated from correct quoted words in descriptions. Agreement with the other two models is the strongest single signal (AUROC \AucXmodelHaiku, \AucXmodelSonnet{} and \AucXmodelOpus{} for Haiku, Sonnet and Opus). The model's own re-reading under the transcription prompt (\AucRereadHaiku, \AucRereadSonnet, \AucRereadOpus) and the Tesseract cross-check (\AucOcrHaiku, \AucOcrSonnet, \AucOcrOpus) follow. Tesseract reads Ukrainian poorly on degraded images, but it does not share the language prior of the evaluated models. Lexicon membership (\AucLexHaiku, \AucLexSonnet, \AucLexOpus) and hedging cues (\AucHedgeHaiku, \AucHedgeSonnet, \AucHedgeOpus) are weak. The lexicon fails because hallucinations are often real words. Because the peers in the cross-model signal come from the same vendor, this signal may be optimistic (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

The signals are complementary. The cross-validated combination reaches AUROC \AucCombCIHaiku{} for Haiku, \AucCombCISonnet{} for Sonnet and \AucCombCIOpus{} for Opus, against base rates of \BaseHaiku\%, \BaseSonnet\% and \BaseOpus\%. It catches \RuleRecHaiku\%, \RuleRecSonnet\% and \RuleRecOpus\% of hallucinated words while flagging \RuleFfHaiku\%, \RuleFfSonnet\% and \RuleFfOpus\% of correct ones. The re-reading comparison alone catches \RangeRereadRec\%.

\subsection{Near-word probe on real documents}\label{sec:probe_res}

On the \NProbePairs{} probe pairs, the four models quote the unedited real word correctly on \RangePrOrig\% of the original pages. On the edited pages they quote the printed near-word on only \RangePrNear\%, and on \RangePrNorm\% they quote the real word instead (Haiku \PrNormHaiku\%, Sonnet \PrNormSonnet\%, Opus \PrNormOpus\%, Fable \PrNormFable\%). For every model, the drop from reading the original correctly to reading the near-word correctly is significant (exact McNemar on the pairs: Haiku $p\PrPHaiku$, Sonnet $p\PrPSonnet$, Opus $p\PrPOpus$, Fable $p\PrPFable$). The edit is clearly visible at the probe's resolution (the target letters are at least 11~px high). Prior-driven normalisation is therefore not an artefact of synthetic rendering: on real official documents, the models report the word they expect.

\subsection{Synthetic versus real stimuli}\label{sec:setcompare}

The two sets differ in ways that matter for interpretation. Real stimuli carry \RealTokPerImg{} ground-truth tokens per image against \SynTokPerImg{} in Study~1, with similar word length (mean \RealTokLen{} versus \SynTokLen{} letters). Names and rare words make up \RealNameShare\% of real tokens against \SynNameShare\% of synthetic ones, and digits \RealNumShare\% against \SynNumShare\%. Study~1 thus under-represents rare words and over-represents digits. The invented names of Study~1 over-represent the letters specific to Ukrainian relative to natural Ukrainian text (OpenSubtitles frequency list): \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ї} by a factor of \OverYi, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{є} by \OverYe{} and \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ґ} by \OverGhe. In the real texts, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ґ} is almost absent (\RealGheShare\% of letters). The Ukrainian-letter error rate of Study~1 should therefore be read as a stress test, not as an estimate for everyday text. In Study~2, Ukrainian-letter substitutions make up only \RealSubUaHaiku\% (Haiku), \RealSubUaSonnet\% (Sonnet), \RealSubUaOpus\% (Opus) and \RealSubUaFable\% (Fable) of the hallucinated words, although they do occur on real leaflets (Figure~\ref{fig:failures}). Lexicalisations make up \RealRangeSubLex\% and fabrications \RealRangeSubFab\%. The main effects that Study~1 identified are reproduced on real images: hallucinated words in a majority of responses, unhedged errors, normalisation of near-words, degradation-driven misreading, and a usable combined detector. Their magnitudes differ.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the error profile means for blind users}

The headline token-level rates of a few per cent understate the accessibility problem. A blind user does not receive tokens but descriptions. Between \RangeImg\% of the synthetic descriptions and \RealRangeImg\% of the descriptions of real packaging and documents contained at least one word that is not printed or not visible. Most of these words were delivered without any cue of uncertainty. The errors also concentrate where they hurt. Names and rare words, which a language prior cannot predict, are hallucinated more often than common words in both studies, and they are what a user needs to tell one medicine, bill or product from another. Near-words are reported as the real word they resemble, on synthetic labels and on real official documents alike. A user told that a leaflet says ``\foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{тяжким}'' has no way to learn that it says something else. The rare case in which an unusual printed string matters is the case the models are least likely to report faithfully.

Two further patterns are specific to the Ukrainian setting. First, letters that distinguish Ukrainian from Russian orthography are sometimes replaced by spellings shared with Russian. This happens on real medicine leaflets too, although less often than our stress test with synthetic invented names suggests. The change is easy to miss in a screen-reader rendering, but it alters the spelling of names that users may have to repeat, type or search for. Second, the models describe text that is cut off by the frame edge or covered by a finger by completing it. Completions are usually hedged and often right, but they are not readings. Because aiming errors are frequent in photographs taken by blind people~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz}, a description that fills in the unseen part removes the cue that would prompt the user to re-take the photograph.

Within the one model family we tested, capability is no remedy. The most expensive model, Fable~5.1, produced a hallucinated word in significantly more descriptions than the mid-range models in both studies. It tends to say more and to complete more of what is hidden, and all four models share the prior-driven normalisation of unfamiliar words. Whether the same holds for other vendors' models is open. Within this family, choosing a larger model does not replace a run-time check.

\subsection{Design recommendations}

The results suggest the following measures for assistive applications that read text with models of this kind:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Re-read before reading out.} A second, constrained transcription call by the same model is the cheapest single check. For Haiku it adds \RealCostHaikuTrans{}~USD and about \RealCostHaikuTransSec{}~s per real image (Table~\ref{tab:cost}). Comparing the two readings word by word catches \RangeRereadRec\% of the hallucinated words in Study~1. Adding an on-device OCR pass, which shares no language prior with the model, and a second model where available raises this to \RangeRuleRec\% at a false-flag rate of \RangeRuleFf\%. On real images the gains are smaller (Table~\ref{tab:realdetect}), and a second model at least doubles cost and latency.
\item \emph{Mark, do not hide.} Flagged words should be voiced with an explicit marker (e.g., ``probably \ldots'') or spelled out letter by letter, rather than removed. This lets the user decide whether to re-take the photograph or seek sighted verification, in line with the verification strategies blind users already employ~\cite{alharbi2024misfitting,xie2025beyond}.
\item \emph{Separate reading from inference.} Prompts and interfaces should distinguish ``the label says'' from ``the hidden part is probably''. Our transcription prompt shows that completions can be almost eliminated by instruction. The description prompt should therefore ask the model to state explicitly where the frame cuts the text, which also tells the user how to move the camera.
\item \emph{Treat names and numbers as critical.} Digits and names should always pass the cross-check and, when flagged, be offered for character-level reading.
\end{enumerate}
These recommendations follow from measured detector performance, not from tests with users. Whether they improve decisions is a question for a user study (below).

\subsection{Limitations}

\emph{One vendor.} All evaluated models are Claude models of one vendor, queried through its command-line client with default settings. No statement in this paper is about vision-language models in general. Other model families, decoding settings and on-device models may behave differently. In particular, the cross-model detector may be optimistic here, because the peer models share a vendor and plausibly some of their errors. With independent vendors' models it may perform worse or better. A multi-vendor replication on the released stimuli is the most important next step. Fable~5.1 received only the description prompt, so it is not part of the detection analysis.

\emph{Synthetic versus real stimuli.} Study~1 is fully synthetic. Section~\ref{sec:setcompare} quantifies how it differs from real text: about six times fewer words per image, fewer rare words, more digits, and invented names that over-represent \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ї}, \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{є} and especially \foreignlanguage{ukrainian}{ґ} relative to natural Ukrainian. Its absolute rates, in particular the Ukrainian-letter error rate, are therefore not estimates for everyday text. Study~2 replaces the content with real packaging and documents, but its degradations (blur, cropping, finger and glare) are still applied synthetically. Its documents are renderings of PDF pages laid on a surface, without folds, curvature or real lighting. Its packaging photographs were taken by sighted contributors and are better framed than typical photographs taken by blind people~\cite{gurari2018vizwiz}. The near-word edit is typeset in a generic serif face, and an attentive reader may notice it.

\emph{Ground truth.} Each Study~2 genre has its own reference: Google Cloud Vision OCR checked against an independent contributor transcription for packaging, and the PDF text layer checked against Tesseract for documents. Both can miss text that is drawn rather than typeset (logos, stamps) or misread decorative type, and a model that correctly reads such text is scored as fabricating. Signage photographs were collected but excluded, because no reference independent of the evaluated model family was available. The automated audit indicates how large the resulting overstatement is.

\emph{Labelling and audit.} Claimed text is identified by quotation marks, which the prompt requested. Text a model reads out without quoting it is not scored, and quoted text that a model presents as an alternative reading is scored as a claim. The audit used two automated raters, not people. One rater belongs to the evaluated family, and the other, an open 12-billion-parameter model, could not resolve single-letter differences in Ukrainian and agreed poorly with the first. A human audit by Ukrainian readers remains necessary for a validated error rate.

\emph{Stability and scale.} Individual responses vary between runs (Section~\ref{sec:stability}), so per-image results are noisy. Aggregate rates are stable, but the run-to-run check covered only \StabN{} images. Study~2 has fewer than 20 images per model in each (quality, framing, genre) cell, so its condition-level comparisons are underpowered and mostly not significant after correction.

\emph{No user study.} We measure what is said, not how blind users perceive or act on it. Hallucination rates are proxies for the risk of acting on wrong information, not measures of harm. How flagged words should be voiced, and whether flags improve decisions without eroding trust in correct readings, requires a study with blind participants using screen readers in realistic reading tasks. Task-based usability instruments of the kind developed for adapted reading materials could support such a study~\cite{zatserkovnyi2023usability}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Four Claude models read Ukrainian signs, labels and documents well enough that per-word error rates are low. They nevertheless assert text that is not printed in a large share of their descriptions: in \RangeImg\% of descriptions of synthetic stimuli and \RealRangeImg\% of descriptions of real packaging and documents. The main mechanisms are normalising unusual words to familiar ones, which we reproduced on real official documents with a one-letter edit, altering digits, completing text the camera did not capture and, less often on real text, replacing Ukrainian-specific letters. Most of these assertions carry no hedge, and the most capable model of the family is not the most faithful reader. The errors are detectable at run time. A second constrained reading, an independent OCR pass and agreement between models together separate hallucinated from correct words with an AUROC of \RangeAucComb{} on synthetic and \RealRangeAucComb{} on real images. Assistive applications can therefore tell blind users \emph{which} words to doubt. Whether these findings hold for other vendors' models, and whether such flags help users, remains to be tested. The stimulus generator, source lists, ground truth and analysis code are released to support both.
